# Story structure for very short films (30-120 s, up to ~5 min): film schools and canonical literature

Scope note: ~20 tool calls. Primary sources were reached for Pixar's 22 rules (Coats via Aerogramme), Cowgill's own "Ten Rules" text, Harmon's own Channel 101 tutorial (archived), Mackendrick's CalArts handouts (The Sticking Place extract), USC course catalogue entries, and the Wikipedia article on kishōtenketsu. Nash, Cooper & Dancyger, and Claudia Johnson were only reached through publisher pages, tables of contents and summaries; their exact page-level wording is NOT quoted here.

## 1. What film-school short-film curricula teach

### Takeaway
Schools treat the short as its own form, taught in a dedicated course (USC CTWR 413/513, NYU via Cooper & Dancyger), with hard scope constraints (90 s, no dialogue, 2 characters, 1 simple set, under 3 min) and dramatic-construction basics: every scene shifts the balance, cause and effect, exposition only inside present tension. Little school-specific material was findable for AFI, UCLA, FAMU, La Fémis, Beijing Film Academy or NFTS.

### Cited Findings
- USC School of Cinematic Arts runs CTWR 413 "Writing the Short Script I" (2 units), catalogue description "Preparation of scripts for short films: form, structure, planning"; Fall 2015 sections were taught by Robert Sabin, Deborah Baron and Paul De Meo — [USC Schedule of Classes](https://classes.usc.edu/term-20153/course/ctwr-413)
- USC also lists CTWR 513 "Writing the Short Script" (graduate, first semester), CTWR 505 "Creating the Short Film" (MFA Production requirement) and CTWR 215 "Practicum in Writing Short Films" — [USC catalogue](https://catalogue.usc.edu/content.php?catoid=18&navoid=7157&p3909=5); [USC CTWR course list via Coursicle](https://www.coursicle.com/usc/courses/CTWR/)
- A Spring 2021 USC SCA 2-unit syllabus (only a preview seen) states goals of learning screenwriting fundamentals and writing a short script; 60% weekly assignments, 30% final script — [Scribd syllabus](https://www.scribd.com/document/718268622/19183) (preview only; week-by-week content not seen)
- NYU Tisch: Ken Dancyger is Professor of Film and Television at Tisch; he and Patricia Cooper (both NYU teachers) wrote "Writing the Short Film" (Focal Press 1994; 3rd ed. 2004/2005), covering scripts of 30 minutes or less — [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Writing-the-Short-Film/Cooper-Dancyger/p/book/9780240805887); [O'Reilly TOC](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/writing-the-short/9780240805887/)
- CalArts: Alexander Mackendrick ran the CalArts film school 1969-1978 and taught until his death in 1993; "On Film-making" (2004, ed. Paul Cronin) collects his CalArts handouts on "dramatic construction" and "film grammar" — [The Sticking Place](https://thestickingplace.com/alexander-mackendrick/); [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/875419.On_Film_making)
- Mackendrick's first-day line to students: "Student films come in three sizes, too long, much too long, and very much too long" — [The Sticking Place / summary of On Film-making](https://thestickingplace.com/alexander-mackendrick/) (quoted second-hand in search summaries; book page not seen)
- Mackendrick: comic structure is "simply dramatic structure but MORE SO: neater, shorter, faster" (p. 41); exposition is boring unless delivered inside a present tension or crisis (p. 41); "what is happening NOW is not as exciting as what may or may not HAPPEN NEXT" (pp. 41-42); slogan "BEWARE OF FLASHBACKS, DREAM SEQUENCES AND VISIONS" — [summary pages of On Film-making](https://thestickingplace.com/alexander-mackendrick/dramatic-construction/) (page numbers come from a secondary summary; verify against the book)
- Mackendrick valued Sarcey's definition of drama, "expectation mingled with uncertainty", and stressed the audience's implicit question "What Happens Next?"; also "There are no rules. There never were and there never will be." — [The Sticking Place: Dramatic Construction](https://thestickingplace.com/alexander-mackendrick/dramatic-construction/)
- The Sticking Place's Mackendrick-derived short exercises include "The Beggar's Cup", a three-act vignette framed as equilibrium → unbalancing → apparent restoration, and a version told with only ten words of dialogue — [The Sticking Place: Dramatic Construction](https://thestickingplace.com/alexander-mackendrick/dramatic-construction/)
- CalArts BFA Character Animation: each student makes "one short, individually-made film each year"; year one covers storytelling and character animation, year two adds dialogue, sound effects and music — [CalArts BFA Character Animation](https://calarts.edu/academics/programs-and-degrees/bfa-character-animation)
- CalArts admission looks for "potential to tell stories and develop characters using sequential imagery" (storyboards, comics, flipbooks...) — [CalArts admission requirements](https://calarts.edu/apply/bfa-character-animation)
- CalArts first-year film brief (student account, 2015): a 90-second animated film, no dialogue, black and white; premises are tiny, e.g. "A raccoon finds a flashlight in the woods" — [Christine Snitkjaer blog](https://christinesnitkjaer.blogspot.com/2015/10/creating-my-own-film_11.html) (student blog, second-hand)
- University of Washington CSE 456 (Story for Digital Animation): no more than two characters, a simple inside set, story told in under 3 minutes; pitch with a beat sheet and thumbnails — [UW CSE 456](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse456/14su/assignments/assignment_3)
- BYU senior animated film pitches: a single simple paragraph, ~3 min film, 2-3 characters, 2-3 environments; a BYU director: "They love the visuals, but when I explain the plot, it's kind of like, 'Huh?'" — [BYU TMA pitches](https://tma.byu.edu/senior-animated-group-film-pitches); [Y Magazine](https://magazine.byu.edu/?p=17045)

### Inferences
- The consistent film-school constraint set for a ~90-180 s animated short is: ≤2 characters, 1 (max 2-3) environments, little or no dialogue, a premise statable in one sentence. Our 30-120 s pixel-diorama episodes should fit this envelope.
- Mackendrick's "exposition only under present tension" maps directly onto a cold open: start in the problem, explain later through action/dialogue boxes.
- "Students' films are too long" suggests cutting every beat that does not shift the balance.

### Gaps
- No syllabus or faculty writing on short structure found for AFI Conservatory, UCLA TFT, FAMU Prague, La Fémis, Beijing Film Academy or NFTS UK in the searches run (only one NFTS-targeted search; zero results).
- USC CTWR 413 week-by-week content and textbook list not visible (Scribd preview only).

## 2. Canonical frameworks and how they scale down to 1-3 minutes

### Takeaway
Frameworks that are causal and scale-free (McKee's scene-as-value-turn, Pixar/Adams story spine, Harmon's circle, which was born at a 5-minute festival, kishōtenketsu from four-line poems) shrink well to 30-120 s. Page-pegged feature beat sheets (Save the Cat's 15 beats on 110 pages, Syd Field's plot points) have no sourced short-film endorsement; critics limit Save the Cat even within features.

### Cited Findings
**McKee, "Story" (1997)**
- Scene definition: "A SCENE is an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value with a degree of perceptible significance." Check: value charge at the top vs. end of the scene — [McKee, Story (archive.org PDF)](https://ia601609.us.archive.org/31/items/robert-mc-kee-story-pdf/Robert_McKee_Story_pdf.pdf); [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/robert-mckee-story-structure/)
- Inciting incident: the first major event that "radically upsets the balance of forces in the protagonist's life", arouses desire to restore balance, raises the Major Dramatic Question; timing: execute it the instant the audience understands character and world enough to react; the climax answers the question it raised — [Mordego Films: The Inciting Incident by McKee](https://www.mordego.com/screenplay/the-inciting-incident-by-mckee/); [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/robert-mckee-story-structure/)
- "Activity is not action"; turning points produce surprise, increased curiosity, insight, new direction; three major reversals are the minimum for a full-length work — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/robert-mckee-story-structure/); [Mordego: Act Design](https://www.mordego.com/screenplay/2-act-design-by-robert-mckee/)
- No source found where McKee prescribes short-film structure specifically.

**Pixar 22 rules (Emma Coats, 2011) and the Story Spine**
- Emma Coats, then a Pixar story artist, tweeted the rules in 2011; she says they are a compilation of what she learned from Pixar colleagues, not all her own — [Aerogramme](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/); [Pixar Post](https://pixarpost.com/2012/07/former-pixarian-emma-coats-to-speak-at.html)
- Rules most actionable for micro-shorts (verbatim): #4 "Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___."; #5 "Simplify. Focus. Combine characters. Hop over detours."; #6 "What is your character good at, comfortable with? Throw the polar opposite at them."; #7 "Come up with your ending before you figure out your middle."; #13 "Give your characters opinions. Passive/malleable ... is poison to the audience."; #16 "What are the stakes?... Stack the odds against."; #19 "Coincidences to get characters into trouble are great; coincidences to get them out of it are cheating."; #22 "What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling of it?" — [Aerogramme](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/07/pixars-22-rules-of-storytelling/)
- The Story Spine was created by improv teacher Kenn Adams (he dates it to 1991): "I created the Story Spine in 1991..."; full form: Once upon a time / Every day / But one day / Because of that / Because of that / Because of that / Until finally / And ever since then — [Aerogramme: Back to the Story Spine (Adams guest post)](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/06/05/back-to-the-story-spine/); [Improv Encyclopedia](https://improvencyclopedia.org/games/Story_Spine.html); [NPR 2026 interview](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/23/nx-s1-5750619/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)
- Transmission to Pixar is second-hand: a commenter says Rebecca Stockley learned it from Adams and brought it to Pixar ~1997; credit was earlier given to Brian McDonald — [Aerogramme: The Story Spine](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/22/the-story-spine-pixars-4th-rule-of-storytelling/) (comment-level, unverified)
- Scaling claim: use one or two "Because of that" lines for a short, five or more for a feature; if "and then" fits, it is an episode, not a causal link — [FlashBoards](https://flashboards.yaroflasher.com/learn/storytelling/pixar-story-spine/) (secondary, tool-vendor blog)

**Dan Harmon story circle**
- Harmon built it while running Channel 101 (co-founded 2003 with Rob Schrab), a monthly LA festival of pilots under 5 minutes; most submissions "had beginnings and endings but nothing connecting the two" — [Loreteller](https://loreteller.com/learn/story-circle-explained/) (secondary); [Wikipedia: Dan Harmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Harmon)
- Eight steps: You (comfort zone) → Need → Go (unfamiliar situation) → Search → Find → Take (pay a price) → Return → Change. Primary tutorial: "Story Structure 101: Super Basic Shit" — [Channel 101 wiki archive](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit); [Bang2write](https://bang2write.com/2020/01/spotlight-on-structure-the-story-circle-harmon.html)
- Harmon: the model "won't fit every single story... There are exceptions to everything, but that's called style, not structure" — [Bang2write paraphrase](https://bang2write.com/2020/01/spotlight-on-structure-the-story-circle-harmon.html)

**Kishōtenketsu (East Asian four-part, includes Vietnamese)**
- Four parts: ki (introduction), shō (development), ten (twist/reversal), ketsu (conclusion); Chinese qǐchéngzhuǎnhé from four-line Qijue poetry (Fan Heng, 1272-1330); used in Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Vietnamese works — [Wikipedia: Kishōtenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- Vietnamese form: khởi/khai – thừa – chuyển – hợp, associated with tứ tuyệt poems; "hợp" concludes the idea of the whole poem. Korean: giseungjeongyeol — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- Classic example (Rai Sanyō): ki: the Itoya daughters of Honmachi, Osaka; shō: elder 16, younger 14; ten: lords killed with bows and arrows; ketsu: "The daughters of Itoya kill with their eyes" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- It is popularly called "without conflict", but works using it, e.g. Parasite, "can and often do contain conflict" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/what-is-kishotenketsu)
- Celtx claims it suits short films and visual storytelling and does not require an antagonist, a central goal or escalating obstacles; shō runs parallel to the opening rather than escalating — [Celtx blog](https://blog.celtx.com/what-is-kishotenketsu-defined/) (vendor blog); can be nested at sequence level in longer works — same source
- Used in yonkoma (four-panel) manga — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)

**Save the Cat / Syd Field / Truby**
- Save the Cat (2005) pegs 15 beats to specific pages of a ~110-page script; criticised as overly formulaic — [Wikipedia: Save the Cat!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Save_the_Cat!:_The_Last_Book_on_Screenwriting_You'll_Ever_Need)
- Daniel Calvisi: the beat sheet "was designed for his genre which is commercial family comedies or high concept comedies" — [Film Courage / Medium](https://medium.com/film-courage/the-big-problem-with-save-the-cat-is-that-it-doesnt-apply-to-every-genre-daniel-calvisi-9b8ae6625590)
- Erik Bork: "bad Save the Cat scripts" are those where the beats are obvious (theme stated p.5, catalyst p.12, break into two p.25) — [Flying Wrestler](https://www.flyingwrestler.com/2024/07/save-the-cat-is-not-always-right/)

### Inferences
- For 30-120 s: use McKee at the scene level (1-3 value turns total), the story spine with ONE or TWO "because of that" links, or kishōtenketsu (four beats, ~4 dialogue-box groups). Harmon's 8 steps are viable at the upper end (~2-5 min) since they were made for <5 min pilots, but 8 beats in 60 s is ~7 s per beat, which is tight.
- Kishōtenketsu is a natural fit for a Vietnamese audience (khởi-thừa-chuyển-hợp is taught through tứ tuyệt poetry) and for gag/slice-of-life episodes that do not need an antagonist; the "chuyển" (ten) beat should recontextualise the first two.
- Save the Cat and Syd Field's page-pegged paradigm do not scale to a 1-minute short in any source found; treat their beat names (catalyst, midpoint) as vocabulary only.

### Gaps
- No primary Syd Field or John Truby text found on short films (not searched in depth; no citation available). Truby's "Anatomy of Story" seven key steps are not covered here.
- No expert statement found that explicitly says "X fails at 1-3 minutes"; failure claims above are inferences.

## 3. Short-film-specific books (Nash, Cowgill, Cooper & Dancyger, Johnson)

### Takeaway
The short-film literature converges on: one main conflict, set up in the first minute, escalation, an optional midpoint reversal, and an ending (reversal/reveal/payoff) that is the best thing in the film. Under 5 minutes, one type of conflict is enough (Cowgill).

### Cited Findings
- Linda Cowgill, "Ten Rules for Writing a Successful Short Script" (verbatim, from her filmmakeriq post): #4 "The best ideas are simple. Focus on one main conflict, develop and explore it in surprising ways."; #5 "Set your film up in the first 60 seconds... Establish your conflict as soon as possible."; #6 "Make sure conflict escalates. Know what your character wants (the goal) and what's preventing him from getting it (the obstacle)"; #7 "develop the conflict in one main incident as the set piece... exploring character in ways feature films rarely do"; #8 "If your film is less than 5 minutes, one type of conflict might be sufficient... over 5 minutes, you're going to need various obstacles or complications"; #9 "Just because your film is short doesn't mean you can't have an effective mid-point and reversal. Anything that keeps your audience from guessing your ending is an asset."; #10 "Make sure your ending is the best thing about your great film. Your payoff is what you're leaving the audience with" — [Cowgill Ten Rules PDF (copy of filmmakeriq.com 2009 post)](http://fergusonfilm.weebly.com/uploads/7/5/1/8/75184707/ten_rules_screenwriting.pdf)
- Cowgill, "Ten Ways to Strengthen Your Plot": "Characters are defined by the choices they make"; "Use cause and effect plotting to make sure each scene leads believably to the next"; "The best surprises are the reversal and the reveal... Reversals work best when the audience has been set up for one result and get the opposite. Reveals work best when the revelation has been cleverly foreshadowed early, but not given away." — [same PDF, page 2](http://fergusonfilm.weebly.com/uploads/7/5/1/8/75184707/ten_rules_screenwriting.pdf)
- Cowgill's book "Writing Short Films: Structure and Content for Screenwriters" (Lone Eagle 1997; 2nd ed. 2005) has a chapter "The Three Part Nature of Film Structure" and uses "Life Lessons" (Price/Scorsese) as its main example; quoted rule: "There must be: A character (the protagonist), who will take action to achieve something. This character will meet with conflict." — [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/243512.Writing_Short_Films); [sobrief summary](https://sobrief.com/books/writing-short-films) (summary site, second-hand)
- Cooper & Dancyger: the short "lends itself to experimentation but requires tremendous discipline"; aim to be "brief and complete"; story = narration of how something happened to someone, plus causality = plot; the two most useful structures for short scripts are the oldest narrative forms, "the journey" and "the ritual occasion" — [Perlego excerpt](https://www.perlego.com/book/1627320/writing-the-short-film-pdf); [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Writing-the-Short-Film/Cooper-Dancyger/p/book/9780240805887) (via search summary of excerpt)
- Patrick Nash, "Short Films: Writing the Screenplay" (Kamera Books, 2012, 256 pp.): chapters include "Structure and Length", "Story Is King", "Beginnings, Middles and Ends", "Characters, Goals and Motivation", "Conflict, Obstacles and Stakes", "Eliciting Emotion"; Nash sat on short-film juries at the Foyle Film Festival — [Perlego TOC](https://www.perlego.com/book/1707428/short-films-writing-the-screenplay-pdf); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Short_Films.html?id=k-pK6bX86O0C)
- Nick LaRovere (Raindance): a short should explore "only one major concept or idea", "only one relationship/conflict", and "wrap up... in a clean and satisfying way"; a twist is one effective payoff — [Raindance](https://raindance.org/3-elements-of-short-film-structure-you-need-to-know/)
- No Film School: begin with action then fill in backstory; write to the one location and few actors you have — [No Film School: short story rules](https://nofilmschool.com/short-story-rules-short-film). Blake Ridder (70 shorts): shorts work like magic tricks, control what the audience sees and when; a short needs a relatable character, felt emotion and a hook at the top — [No Film School: Blake Ridder](https://nofilmschool.com/blake-ridder-short-film-advice)
- A twist works when earned, reveals character/theme and changes the meaning of what came before — [bookwritinginc](https://www.bookwritinginc.com/blog/how-to-write-the-story-structure-of-a-short-film/) (low-authority blog)

### Inferences
- Concrete rule set for our episodes: one protagonist with one visible want, one obstacle type, one location (one diorama), one turn; set up within the first ~10 s for a 60 s episode (proportional to Cowgill's "first 60 seconds" of a 10-min film); ending = reversal (set up A, deliver not-A) or reveal (foreshadowed early), designed first (Pixar #7).
- "Journey" and "ritual occasion" (Cooper & Dancyger) suit a diorama: a ritual occasion (a meal, festival, exam day, Tết) gives a built-in single location and timeline.

### Gaps
- Claudia Johnson's "Crafting Short Screenplays" not found in searches; no claims recorded.
- Nash's actual chapter content on structure and length not accessible (TOC only).

## 4. Episodic micro-series (1-2 min episodes): cliffhangers, recurring hooks, cold opens

### Takeaway
The vertical micro-drama industry (ReelShort etc.) is the main documented practice: open IN the conflict within 3-5 s, turn about every 30 s, end on a withheld answer, rotate cliffhanger types. Sources are mostly tool-vendor blogs, not academia.

### Cited Findings
- Micro-dramas: serialized vertical episodes of 1-3 min, 60-100 episodes a season; TheWrap describes ~90-second episodes "each one ending with a juicy cliffhanger" — [Jellypod](https://www.jellypod.com/blog/what-is-a-micro-drama); [Film Threat](https://filmthreat.com/features/what-are-the-characteristics-of-vertical-micro-dramas-key-insights-on-length-and-format/) (TheWrap quote second-hand)
- One-minute beat sheet: hook 0:00-0:03, pressure 0:03-0:20, turn 0:20-0:45, cliffhanger 0:45-1:00 — [character.app](https://www.character.app/blog/what-is-a-vertical-drama)
- 1-2 min episode = 180-360 spoken words in 2-4 scenes, conflict inside 5 s, a turn roughly every 30 s, ends on a hook — [Scenemixer](https://scenemixer.com/guides/vertical-short-drama-script-format/); 60-90 s ≈ 150-250 words, 2-3 characters — [microdrama.id](https://microdrama.id/en/blog/micro-drama-script-example)
- Chinese showrunners call the opening "the explosion point": the episode opens in the conflict, not moving toward it; "Write the button first. Then write backward through the episode to the hook"; a claim attributed to ReelShort's engineering team that holding the freeze-frame at seconds 55-58 retains more viewers — [Axis AI Studios](https://www.axisaistudios.com/blog/script-structure-for-vertical-dramas-episode-by-episode-guide) (vendor blog; ReelShort data not independently verified)
- A cliffhanger is "not a random shock, but a withheld answer"; rotate hook types (identity, emotion, realisation), since two identical hooks in a row feel like a trick — [microdrama.id](https://microdrama.id/en/blog/micro-drama-script-example); [Screenburn](https://screenburn.studio/learn/microdrama-structure)
- Hook must win the viewer within three seconds: "an image that alarms, a line that threatens, a promise that pays" — [Screenburn](https://screenburn.studio/learn/microdrama-structure)
- Harmon's circle is claimed to work for a five-minute short, a sitcom episode, a season arc or a single scene — [Loreteller](https://loreteller.com/learn/story-circle-explained/) (secondary)

### Inferences
- For a series: each episode = one complete mini-turn (Cowgill/McKee) PLUS an open question for the next (micro-drama button). Write the final button/cliffhanger first (matches Pixar #7 and the "write the button first" advice).
- A series-level arc can be laid on Harmon's 8 steps or kishōtenketsu across episodes (e.g. 4 or 8 episodes), with each episode its own small spine.
- 150-250 Vietnamese spoken words per 60-90 s is an industry rule of thumb for English/Chinese scripts; must be re-checked against our own Vbee timing formula (`estimateNarrationSeconds`).

### Gaps
- No academic or film-school source on micro-series structure found; no data on cold opens specifically.
- ReelShort retention figure is second-hand from a vendor blog.

## 5. How animated shorts (Pixar, SparkShorts, Ghibli, CalArts) structure 2-5 minute stories

### Takeaway
Documented process evidence: Pixar SparkShorts start from a personal experience and a single image/character idea, under a six-month limit; CalArts first-year films are 90 s, no dialogue, tiny premises. No primary structural breakdown of Ghibli shorts was found.

### Cited Findings
- SparkShorts announced 18 January 2018: employees get six months and a limited budget; Jim Morris: the program "is designed to discover new storytellers, explore new storytelling techniques, and experiment with new production workflows" — [Wikipedia: A Spark Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Spark_Story); [Disney+ press](https://press.disneyplus.com/disney-plus/sparkshorts/a-spark-story)
- Rosana Sullivan (Kitbull) started from "I just wanted to draw a kitten being a kitten", which grew into a story of connection between two outcasts drawn from her childhood — [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2019/12/kitbull-director-rosana-sullivan-pixar-sparkshorts-interview-news-1202807212/)
- Kristen Lester (Purl): made in six months from conception to finish; the visual idea was built around the material (yarn) — [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2019/11/purl-director-kristen-lester-pixar-sparkshorts-animated-short-interview-1202779200/)
- Searit Huluf (Self): a wooden puppet desperate to be like the shiny metal figures around her (one character, one want, one visual contrast) — [POC Culture](https://pocculture.com/interview-pixar-sparkshorts-film-self-director-searit-huluf/)
- CalArts: one individually-made short per year; first-year brief 90 s without dialogue — [CalArts](https://calarts.edu/academics/programs-and-degrees/bfa-character-animation); [Snitkjaer blog](https://christinesnitkjaer.blogspot.com/2015/10/creating-my-own-film_11.html)
- Mackendrick's handouts reached CalArts students; a second-hand comment says Pixar staff told Rebecca Stockley that CalArts taught a structure similar to the story spine — [Aerogramme comments](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/22/the-story-spine-pixars-4th-rule-of-storytelling/) (unverified)

### Inferences
- The animation-short pattern is: one character, one concrete visual contrast or material (yarn, wood vs. metal, kitten), a want visible on screen, a relationship change by the end. For pixel-art in a diorama, the "material idea" could be the diorama itself (a room that changes) and the JRPG dialogue box (a box that lies, a box that is cut off).
- Animated shorts lean on images over words (Mackendrick film grammar; CalArts no-dialogue brief), so dialogue boxes should carry what the picture cannot, not narrate what it shows.

### Gaps
- No structural analysis of Studio Ghibli shorts (e.g. Ghibli Museum shorts) found; not searched in depth.
- No Pixar official published story-structure document beyond Coats' tweets (which Pixar did not publish as official policy).
